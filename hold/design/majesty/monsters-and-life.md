# Majesty Gold: monsters, lairs and the life of the kingdom

Scope: Majesty: The Fantasy Kingdom Sim (Cyberlore, 2000) plus The Northern Expansion (together Majesty Gold). Written as a "minimum bar" reference for a designer.

How to read the tags:
- Untagged facts come from the official Majesty Gold HD manual (read in full from the Steam PDF) or from the Wikipedia summary.
- (fan) means the claim comes from a player source: Steam forum threads, a GameFAQs-style walkthrough (the Steven W. Carter walkthrough, read via a mirror), blogs and forum posts.
- (unsure) means I could not confirm it, or sources disagree, or I only saw it in a search-result snippet and not on the page itself.

Honest limits of this research:
- The Majesty fandom wiki returned HTTP 402 to every fetch. I did not try to get round that. Anything attributed to it below came from search-engine summaries of its pages, so it is tagged (unsure).
- GameFAQs, TV Tropes, GameSpot and several others returned 403. The same goes for those.
- The manual is thin on per-monster behaviour: it gives a paragraph of lore per monster and a few behavioural hooks. Most of the "what does it actually do" detail below is fan-sourced. Exact numbers (spawn rates, hit points) are mostly fan estimates.
- Reddit threads were not reachable through the search tool, so no Reddit material is used.
- I did not find a source that lists every lair type with its spawn table. Part 1 therefore groups lairs by what players reported, and says where a mapping is a guess.

# Part 1. Monsters, lairs and spawners

## 1.1 The big picture: where monsters come from

The manual gives four sources of trouble, and they behave very differently. A designer should treat these as four separate systems.

1. **Lairs.** Most monsters breed in lairs scattered across the wilderness. The manual says lairs run "from primitive animal dens and warrens to elaborate, dark castles". Fewer lairs means fewer monsters, so destroying them is the root fix. You cannot order anyone to do it. You post an Attack Flag (a gold bounty) on the lair, or you hope a "righteous (some might say crazed)" hero goes on their own. A lair is destroyed by hitting it until it collapses; heroes do not delve inside (Wikipedia, fan).
2. **Wanderers from outside the realm.** Some monsters do not come from lairs at all. They cross in from neighbouring lands. You cannot attack the source, so the manual's advice is to kill as many as you can in the hope of discouraging future immigration. In the freestyle setup screen "Wandering Monsters" is a setting that selects which monster types wander in over time. So this is a time-based trickle you tune, not a fixed script.
3. **Growth-triggered spawners inside your own kingdom.** The manual lists these as "buildings that appear" and says they are "more akin to lairs than buildings": Sewer Entrances and Graveyards. Both are indestructible in the base game and both exist as a direct consequence of your success. The manual calls them "inescapable results of that growth".
4. **Scripted events and waves.** The Northern Expansion manual says quests contain "coordinated waves of monster attacks" and that special events can be switched on in freestyle and multiplayer. After you win an epic quest you may keep playing, and scripts that continue spawning monsters then run automatically. You can also type cheat-style phrases during play that trigger attacks: "Night of the Living Dead", "Prepare to Die", "Pump Up the Volume", "Goblin Rush" and "Give Me Action". (The manual lists these, but what each phrase spawns is (unsure).)

Also worth knowing: the manual stresses monsters carry gold. Heroes "gain money for killing monsters or fulfilling reward flags". Killing monsters is how heroes get rich, and rich heroes spend at your shops and pay tax at their guild. So monsters are part of your economy, not just a threat. One reviewer says heroes wander the realm looking for fights because monsters carry gold they can spend in town. Fans add the counterpoint: gold from lairs is small and one-off compared with a developed market economy (fan).

## 1.2 Spawners in your own kingdom

### Sewer Entrance (base game)
- **Spawn trigger:** appears as your kingdom grows. A fan estimate is one new sewer entrance every four or five buildings constructed (fan, one source; (unsure) on the exact ratio). The Hovels that Gnomes keep spawning therefore indirectly create more sewers (fan).
- **What comes out:** Giant Rats and Ratmen. The manual says Ratmen "leap forth from sewers to waylay Tax Collectors and raid unsuspecting settlements".
- **Targets:** Tax Collectors, Marketplaces, houses and cottages. A fan guide says a group of Ratmen can rob a Marketplace of up to about a thousand gold in ten to fifteen seconds (fan, (unsure) on the number). Another says Ratmen "steal large amounts of gold from marketplaces" and that heroes who kill them take the gold back as their reward (fan).
- **Counters:** a Guard House placed near the sewer entrance distracts Ratmen from the Marketplace, because monsters pick the nearest building. The manual itself suggests a Guard House next to each Marketplace for exactly this reason: it "presents alternate targets for monsters, so that they won't necessarily head straight for your vulnerable cash cows". Pairs of ballista towers beside the entrance also work (fan).
- **Cannot be destroyed.** It is permanent pressure whose rate grows with your size.

### Graveyard (base game)
- **Spawn trigger:** appears when enough heroes have died. Wikipedia says a graveyard appears "after many hero deaths". The manual says it is "more akin to a lair" and "spawns undead monsters" and is indestructible. A fan source says roughly every ten hero deaths, and only once a gravestone has expired (fan, (unsure)). A related fan note: Gnomes die a lot and so are a quick way to make graveyards (fan).
- **What comes out:** Zombies and Skeletons. They attack Tax Collectors and Peasants (fan). Skeletons dodge well and resist missiles; the manual says Skeletons "are resistant to magic and missiles". Zombies are "slow, but attack with surprising force".
- **How the gravestone works:** when a hero dies a gravestone stays on the map for a limited time (higher-level heroes last longer). You can bring them back with Resurrection (Temple of Agrela) or Re-animation (Temple of Krypta) cast on the stone. If you recruit a replacement into the guild slot, the lowest-level gravestone is dismissed. So the dead are a slot-management puzzle.
- **Mausoleum (expansion building):** stores dead heroes and, unlike a Graveyard, does not spawn undead. Wikipedia-style fan summaries say having one prevents graveyards forming until it fills up (fan, (unsure) on exact rule). If it is destroyed it releases a horde of undead. Heroes tied to Agrela or Dauros do not go into the Mausoleum. If there is no guild room when you revive someone, they simply leave the kingdom.

### Broken Sewer Main (expansion)
- Appears in your kingdom "in a manner similar to the Sewers", but can be destroyed, and the manual says it "should be quickly" destroyed. Wikipedia describes it as a Ratmen "beachhead" that appears after too many henchmen die (unsure; the manual does not give the trigger).
- Brings the expansion's Ratmen: Ratman Champions (elite leaders of raiding parties), Ratman Shamans (cast enchantments on allies and disease on enemies, "especially dangerous") and Ratapults (see 1.5).

### Ancient Graveyard (expansion)
- A wilderness lair that spawns undead. Unlike the in-kingdom Graveyard, it can be destroyed. Fans report it mainly spawns vampires and is often safe to ignore (fan).

## 1.3 Lairs and quest-specific spawners

Lair names below are from the Carter walkthrough of the quests (fan). Which monster each lair produces is as the walkthrough reported it. Treat as quest-specific, not a global table.

- **Creature dens / animal dens ("creature lairs"):** the generic early lair. Destroying the first couple can release normal monsters as a surprise (fan). A 100 to 200 gold bounty is enough to draw a Warrior (fan).
- **Ruined Keep:** a higher-tier lair. In the Tomb of the Dragon King quest it produces dragons. Fans suggest 400 to 500 gold and more than one hero (fan). Destroying it last avoids an early flood of dragons (fan).
- **Evil Castle:** produces Medusae in that quest (fan).
- **Dark Castle:** produces Werewolves in that quest (fan).
- **Goblin lairs and Spires:** Hold Off the Goblin Hordes has a large number of goblin lairs (fan, about fifteen, (unsure)). In Spires of Death, five spires spawn spell-using goblins; destroying one triggers a goblin wave from the others, and the spires come back after about four days (fan).
- **Slave Pits (Free the Slaves):** four pits, and destroying one yields reinforcements (fan).
- **Snake Pits (Valley of the Serpents):** many pits. Fans use them as decoy targets to misdirect incoming Medusae (fan).
- **Shard Shrines (Brashnard quest):** seven shrines on the map edges. Each drops a magic item box and spawns creatures when destroyed (fan).
- **Altars to Krolm (Wrath of Krolm):** six altars. Destroying one spawns the Avatar of Krolm (fan).
- **Ancient Barrows (Legendary Heroes):** six barrows. Each destruction causes an earthquake and a wave of enemies; a permanent earthquake begins after about thirty days and eventually destroys your base (fan).
- **Archaic Tombs (Darkness Falls):** two tombs, each releases a Wight (Styx and Stones, about 250 hit points each) when destroyed (fan).
- **Shrine of Evil (Balance of Twilight):** repeatedly sends three Black Phantoms (fan).
- **Witch King's tower and Liche Queen's tower:** guarded by Daemonwood and undead. Destroying the tower summons the boss. The Liche Queen's lair is always in the corner farthest from your start and is surrounded by graveyards (fan).
- **Ring Tower (Quest for the Magic Ring):** guarded by Daemonwood. Destroying it triggers Black Phantoms and turns an ally hostile (fan). The ring site can spawn in nine different positions (fan).
- **Crown Tower (Quest for the Crown):** guarded by two Evil Oculi (fan). Minotaurs rush the player almost immediately (fan).
- **Fortress of Ixmil:** spawns waves of creatures and fires magical attacks; appears with an audio cue (fan).
- **Elven Lair:** has the best loot rating and appears in only one quest (fan, one poster).
- **Poisonous plants and Daemonwood groves:** surround some enemy strongholds and appear randomly in Trade Routes. They block exploration and paths (fan). The manual says multiple Strangleweeds near each other "share a collective, almost animal intelligence".

### What lairs drop and guard
- The manual says "some lairs have hidden caches, which become available when the lair is destroyed." These can contain improved weapons and armour.
- Fans: destroyed spawners can yield enchanted weapons and armour, up to about +7, even for heroes who cannot normally carry gear such as Monks and Wizards. Artifact-style drops (Brashnard sphere parts, the Ring of Healing) are campaign-only (fan, one poster).
- Lair gold: pays once, and "not very influential" compared with markets (fan). One player reports that rogues stop stealing from your buildings while lairs are visible on the map, preferring the lair (fan, anecdotal).
- Lair guards chase fleeing Rogues, which leaves two or three at the reward flag (fan). Lair monsters also interrupt exploring: heroes "flee in terror" when exploring near monsters (fan).
- How to discourage heroes from razing lairs (to keep fodder spawning): there is no mechanism except casting the Sorcerer's Abode spell Change of Heart on the raider, or choosing Lunord heroes who do not raid (fan).

## 1.4 Monster by monster (base game)

The one-line lore is from the manual. Behaviour beyond that is fan-sourced unless stated.

**Giant Rat.** Common in every kingdom. The manual's joke: they are "spies for their larger bipedal cousins", the Ratmen. Come out of sewers. Attack Peasants and Tax Collectors. Weak alone, a hazard in swarms near Marketplaces. A Cultist can charm one (Charm Monster).

**Ratmen.** Described under Sewer Entrance. The manual adds a piece of world-building: all trace back to a gigantic central warren with a population rivalling the greatest kingdoms. Targets: Tax Collectors first, then settlements and Marketplaces; they take gold and heroes who kill them recover it. Fans list them as the main reason to put Guard Houses near sewers. Ratmen run wild in the Liche Queen quest, and sewer Ratmen are the only monsters that attack buildings in the Holy Chalice quest (fan).

**Goblins** (four kinds).
- *Goblin:* weak individually but vicious, fights to the death with short swords; the manual warns that in numbers they form an overwhelming war band. Fans: they are fast and can wreck a building or two before heroes arrive; a goblin-and-rat swarm can rush down a Blacksmith (fan).
- *Goblin Archer:* hunters with crude, heavy arrows.
- *Goblin Priest:* worships Grum-Gog, lord of pestilence, and casts Insect Swarm.
- *Goblin Champion:* veteran who dual-wields.
- *Goblin Overlord (expansion):* big and "intelligent for a Goblin"; aids concentrated attacks and protects specific goblin lairs.

**Giant Spider.** The manual's backstory: a goblin tribe bred them as guardian beasts and the spiders ate their masters. Fans: weak alone, but swarms drop buildings very quickly if they arrive together. One fan says named heroes of the same class react differently: some flee a spider in the Dark Forest while others fight two or three at once (fan, see Part 2).

**Troll.** The manual: tough, slowly regenerates damage, "enjoys treasure as much as the average Rogue", and is "often found waylaying Caravans or attacking Marketplaces". Fans: Trolls go for Marketplaces and for gold stored in Trading Posts; gold left in Trading Post coffers attracts Trolls, Rogues, Tax Collectors and Ratmen (fan). Guard Houses next to Marketplaces buy time. Gnomes, who build fast, can sometimes repair a Marketplace faster than a Troll wrecks it (fan). Poisoned weapons from a level 2 Rogues Guild counter regeneration (fan). Ballista towers handle Trolls well and Ballistae do not rise in price (fan).

**Minotaur.** The manual: roam in raiding parties. Fans: groups of about five; lone wanderers head to the nearest Trading Post; early waves hit outlying houses on the way to the Palace; three Minotaurs arrive around the time you finish your first defences on certain expert maps (fan). The manual's fiction has a Wizard guild using lightning storms on a Minotaur herd sacking a Marketplace. A Lightning Storm from the Wizards Guild is the stock answer for three or more (fan). Single Minotaurs can destroy houses and can kill a Temple to Agrela if they wander there (fan). Minotaurs rush the player at the start of Quest for the Crown, so you upgrade the Wizards Guild early (fan).

**Harpy.** Spawn of the serpent queen Scrylia that rebelled against her. Flying. A search-engine summary says they "fly around stealing gold" (unsure). Fans call them relatively weak and note Harpies carry out a second raid in the Slay the Mighty Dragon quest (fan).

**Medusa.** Core of Scrylia's legions; "seeking to bring their own reign of terror to Ardania". Fans: high magic resistance, chase heroes and Elves (fan). Greater Gorgons (expansion) travel with them; the manual says they "can petrify" humans, and fans say Gorgons turn heroes to stone and leave them vulnerable, so use bounties on Gorgons to protect single heroes (fan).

**Dryad.** Fey spirits that "prey on Humans, though none know why". A search-engine summary says they prey on humans who stray too close to their lairs (unsure).

**Daemonwood.** Ancient tree spirits that hate humans (they use wood for building and burning). Fans: huge hit-point pool, hits hard, hard to see; guards rings, towers and diagonal paths (fan). Treated as a coin-flip against a Warrior of Discord (fan). Rock Golem and Daemonwood are "nature spirits" sharing a theme.

**Rock Golem.** Slow, hits hard, vulnerable to magic. Fans: about 290 hit points; it "will prioritise attacking buildings" (unsure); the late-game mob in the Fertile Plain; usually attacks alone; three ballistae can kill one; Lightning Storms handle waves of them (fan). Rogues on "defend home" with fire balm stop them (fan).

**Hell Bear.** Foul tempered; "savagely attack any heroes who stray too close to the bears' lairs", with the remains of victims strewn around dens. A Cultist can charm one, and at level 7 can take hell bear form (fan).

**Varg.** Evil wolf relatives. Cultists can charm them (fan). A large pack can cost several game days (fan).

**Werewolf.** A human with a lycanthropic disease, in permanent homicidal rage. In the Tomb of the Dragon King quest a Dark Castle produces them (fan). Reviewers note that "weak" heroes sometimes unexpectedly beat Werewolves or Minotaurs (Genesis Temple comment, fan).

**Zombie.** Slow, hits hard. **Skeleton.** Resistant to magic and missiles, good at dodging. Both come from Graveyards, the Liche Queen and Priestesses of Krypta. Killers of Tax Collectors and Peasants (fan).

**Vampire.** The most feared undead; created by Krypta in her first flush of power. Casts Life Leech and Magic Mirror. Fans: arrive singly or in groups of up to five; Magic Mirror reflects Wizard spells and can kill the Wizard who cast them; they are produced by Graveyards, Ancient Graveyards and the Liche Queen; they gather at the back of the Liche Queen map and hinder exploration. Counters named by players: Paladins, the level 2 Anti-Magic Shield from the Wizards Guild, Wither from the Temple of Krypta, enough Rangers or Rogues to out-damage their healing, Healers' Harm Undead, or Invisibility so enemies can't set up Magic Mirror (fan). Five-vampire groups can kill level 7+ Paladins (fan).

**Roc.** Evil parody of Ardania's eagle that "tend to prey on the weak and infirm". Flying. Cultists can charm them (fan).

**Rust Spitter.** Dissolves food with acid; extremely territorial, rarely met in groups of fewer than three.

**Evil Oculus** (fans spell it "beholder"/"occulus"). Newer menace, maybe related to Dragons. Casts Paralytic Gaze and Electrical Fury. Guards the Crown Tower in pairs and appears as shrine guard and from some lairs. Can kill Gnomes at any level (fan).

**Flowering Strangleweed.** Plant monster from a Wizard's misguided magic. Groups share a hive-like awareness. Blocks paths.

**Dragon.** Seldom leaves its nest, but "takes to the air in a fury of fire and death". Fans: Dragons often attack Peasants and workers doing repairs; ranged, so need more ballistae; dwarven static defences, Dwarves, Paladins and Priestesses are the favoured answers; Skeletons soak dragon damage well while Priestess life drain kills it (fan). Dragons roam randomly and can kill a Temple to Agrela (fan). Vendral, the boss dragon in Slay the Mighty Dragon, attacks the Wizards Guild, a temple, the Palace or an Adept unpredictably, and gets stuck attacking a Dwarven Fortress if he spawns on the far side (fan). Heroes fighting Dragons come away weakened and vulnerable to Ratmen (fan).

## 1.5 Expansion monsters (Northern Expansion)

From the manual's list, with fan behaviour added.
- **Yeti:** "one of the deadliest creatures of the Northlands", fists "crush plate armor like paper". Fan: heroes tend to solo Yetis and die, so use bounties to bring groups; Wizards and Rangers behind Guard Houses blast them (fan).
- **Ice Dragon:** like a Dragon but breathes a freezing blast. Fans: high defence, low magic resistance.
- **Greater Gorgon:** see Medusa.
- **Goblin Overlord:** see Goblins.
- **Ratapult:** a siege engine the Ratmen wheel up from the sewers. Controlled by a small driver. Fires Balefire, an explosive magical projectile. This is the expansion's "work together" monster: the manual says new monsters "work together to cause greater mayhem... casting spells on each other to using a nasty siege weapon".
- **Ratman Champion / Shaman:** elite leader and support caster.
- **Shadow Beast:** nearly undetectable fast undead that claw through strong armour; "pets" of an unknown master. A Priestess can charm them. Fans: herds, about 50 hit points each, easy for most heroes (fan, (unsure) whether that applies to all).
- **Black Phantom, Rrongol, Abomination, Url-Shekk, Cyclops (Dirgo), Wights, Avatar of Krolm, Witch King, Liche Queen:** named in fan quest guides (fan). Black Phantoms resist magic and have a nasty fire attack; heroes tend to flee them and often fail to deliver items. Ballista towers are the reliable answer. They are "very suicidal" and enemy heroes can draw them away (fan). Avatar of Krolm has about 4,000 hit points on first appearance, summons weak creatures, and leaves after enough damage (fan). The Witch King continuously spawns spiders and resists Magic Mirror; warriors are the better answer (fan).
- The manual adds that some base-game monsters "have learned new tricks from monsters in the Northlands" in the expansion. It does not say which. (unsure)

## 1.6 Danger over the life of a quest

How danger changes (fan, patterned from the walkthrough and Steam threads, plus the manual's pacing remark):
- **Start:** relaxed. You have time to plan, and the map is dark. Your first money comes from a Marketplace. An early Guard House and cheap defenders matter more than heroes of quality. A half-built Warriors Guild can be torn down by early monsters (fan). Rangers' Guilds build fast and are favoured to be first.
- **First waves:** goblins, rats, a group of Minotaurs or Trolls depending on the quest. "Hordes arrive early", so recruiting quickly matters (Genesis Temple).
- **Growth tax:** each sewer entrance and each graveyard is a permanent drain that scales with your size.
- **Mid-game:** lairs scouted by Rangers get razed by Warriors, Paladins, Solarii and Warriors of Discord on their own, often before the expensive buildings that were meant to use their loot are even built (Gameindustry review). Wandering monsters become tougher types.
- **Late:** boss towers, Dragons, Yetis, Rock Golems, Vampire packs, Black Phantoms. One-hit-wonder approaches (many heroes plus a big bounty) work less well; the answer is spells (Lightning Storm, Gate, Anti-Magic Shield), static defences (ballista towers, Dwarven Settlements) and Paladins/Priestesses.
- **Post-victory:** with the "continue playing" option, scripted monster spawning keeps running.

# Part 2. The life of the kingdom

## 2.1 What reviewers and fans say Majesty is

- **You are a ruler, not a commander.** You cannot give orders. You post flags with gold on them and watch. Wikipedia: "The player is the ruler, not a commander." Reviewer Gamesfirst: you bribe heroes with reward or explore flags and the more you spend, the likelier they are to go. A Gameindustry reviewer liked that it avoids the "tank rush". Dragon magazine compared it to being a Dungeon Master.
- **A toy as much as a game.** One RPG.net reviewer, who nearly gave up in the early going, came to describe it as a toy more than a conventional game, with "extreme variance among characters" giving it its feel.
- **Charm and the personality system.** Fan posts treat Majesty 1 as uniquely charming; one poster refers to its "decision tree/hero personality system" as the model other games fail to match. Another says nothing since has had "the charm of the first game" (RPG Codex thread, fan).
- **The games designer's goal.** Designer Jim DuBois (Genesis Temple interview) says he wanted to avoid micromanaging an army, and that the model comes from his AD&D background and his work on Heroes of Might and Magic II. The Settlers inspired the idea that the player's imagined stories about the subjects should matter as much as the game's events.
- **Not all praise.** Gamesfirst: the campaign is short, repetitive, and kill-everything or make-money. Gameindustry: slowdown with many units. Some early reviewers called its scenarios impossible or its goals unclear.
- **A calm start.** A fan on Steam says each quest starts relaxed and gives time to plan, and that the player "tells the herald" what is wanted without drag-box-and-click (fan).

## 2.2 Hero behaviours players remember

### Money and temptation
- **Heroes have a price.** You cannot order a hero to do anything. A reward flag has to be larger than the "rigors of the distance" and the danger, and "each hero's thirst for gold is different" (manual). Players report: Rogues, Elves and Dwarves answer most flags of 100+ gold; Rangers answer 100+ explore flags but not always 100+ attack flags; Solarii need about 200 for explore flags; Rangers can be "stingy" and need 400 to 600 on some far flags; Barbarians will walk across the map along a line of 200-gold flags and die far from home (fan).
- **Flags are one-way money.** Once placed, a flag cannot be moved or reduced. If you cancel it, the gold is lost, not refunded (manual). Increases come in steps of 100.
- **A flag can be claimed by one hero only.** Explore flags pay one hero; if that hero is distracted by combat, another may take over (fan).
- **Some heroes find money crass.** A reviewer notes some heroes skip rewards entirely (Gamesfirst). A Steam fan says a hero may ignore a flag for ages and then suddenly act bravely.
- **Willpower and vices.** The manual makes this a first-class stat: willpower is "the hero's ability to avoid temptation; piety". Low-willpower heroes waste time and gold in Elven Lounges and Gambling Halls, and those buildings pay no tax. Monks have very high willpower and "will almost never" go into Gambling Halls or Elven Lounges.
- **Elves and gambling.** The manual says Elves have a "weakness for the pleasure-drenched lifestyle", are "strongly attracted to financial opportunities", and earn extra money by playing music for the local population (their usual activity: performing at Inns and Marketplaces). When you invite Elves, an Elven Lounge appears on its own. When you have both Rogues and Elves, a Gambling Hall appears on its own. The Gambling Hall also lets you gamble the royal treasury on a roulette wheel (the manual says "try not to bankrupt your kingdom"). The pay-off for tolerating this: Elven Bungalows double (and compound for multiple bungalows) Marketplace income (fan). Fans estimate the real cost of the Lounge is hero time, around 17 seconds per visit, not hero gold (fan). Reviewers and bloggers love the idea that your subjects drift toward "drinking, gambling and vandalising graves" (blog paraphrase of a YouTube reviewer).
- **Rogues rob you too.** Rogues are "obsessed with finding and stealing gold and treasure", and fans report they will steal from your own buildings (except the Palace) if they feel loyal enough not to, tracked by a hidden loyalty check: three statues make it impossible; two statues plus a Royal Garden does too (fan, a poster's reading of the game code, (unsure)). High-income buildings (Marketplace, Fairgrounds, Magic Bazaar) tempt them most; a long tax-collection delay makes it worse. Rogues can also loot carried gold off corpses (fan). Enemy rogues will try to collect bounties even on their own guild (fan).
- **Elves and thieves destroy your unwanted buildings.** Players use rogues to destroy Elven Lounges and Gambling Halls; there is no penalty for that in the base game, but statues stop heroes attacking your own buildings (fan).
- **Rogue Extortion.** The Rogues Guild can "extort" the whole populace into paying taxes at once. The Guild keeps a sizeable cut (about 30% at level 2) (fan).
- **A Warrior/Dwarf/Rogue's equipment habits.** Some classes simply never buy new gear. (fan, forum report).

### Fear, bravery and cowardice
- Smart heroes retreat from a fight going badly and "live to fight another day" (Gameindustry).
- Some flee monsters that are not especially dangerous (Gamesfirst). Heroes hide in Inns when scared, which saves them (fan).
- Variation inside a class: some Warriors flee a Dark Forest spider while others take on two or three (Steam, fan). Paladins are tanky but "flee too readily" (fan).
- A weak hero sometimes beats a Werewolf or Minotaur by luck (fan).
- Berserk: Barbarians are "almost always berserk" and "somewhat thick in the head" (manual). Heroes forced to "defend home" by a sorcerer's lightning or a destroyed guild can go berserk too (fan, quirks).

### Class behaviours worth stealing
All base descriptions are from the manual. Fan additions are tagged.
- **Solarii (Helia).** "Usual activity: exploring". Fans: they spot lairs easily and raid them on sight with no reward flag, unlike Warriors or Paladins; they garrison Guard Houses and help the City Guard defend, heal inside Guard Houses as well as Inns, and level faster than Adepts (fan). One fan disagrees and says they rarely raid. That's a tension in the sources (fan, (unsure)). The wiki summary (via search engine, (unsure)) lists "garrisoning the Guard Houses" as a hidden attribute. The manual also says Guard Houses "provide refuge for any Solarii who are patrolling the area".
- **Priestesses (Krypta).** Poor fighters, low willpower, high intelligence, "usual activity: summoning skeletal familiars", never wear armour. Spells: Drain Life, Animate Skeleton, and at level 7 **Control Undead**, which "causes undead monsters to aid the caster". A search-engine summary of the wiki says it can charm Skeletons, Zombies, Vampires and Shadow Beasts (unsure). Fans: priestesses and their skeleton swarm soak damage so one big hit is mostly wasted; life drain nearly matches a Wizard's damage; Krypta is "most experience overall" in tests; but priestesses are slow and wander off from their skeletons, and "defend home" can send them berserk (fan). The same temple's Re-animate brings a dead hero back where they fell at half hit points.
- **Cultists (Fervus).** Throw knives, sow poisonous plants that Rogues later harvest to poison their weapons. Charm Monster turns one animal monster into a friend (rats, rocs, hellbears, vargs). At level 7 their Change Shape lets them take an animal monster's form (hell bear) (manual, fan).
- **Rangers.** "Usual activity: exploring", fast, good eyesight. After the whole realm is mapped they "sometimes journey to distant lands, disappearing off the edge of the map and appearing again later, if they have survived" (manual). Because they find lairs, they cause Warriors to go raid them (fan).
- **Paladins and Warriors of Discord.** Raid lairs on their own (fan). The latter have a special attack that can kill an enemy in one blow, and wear self-designed spiked leather.
- **Wizards.** Cautious, "usual activity: research"; tend to be slow, fragile and expensive; need a Library for some spells; a Wizard Guild lightning bolt can turn a Wizard berserk (fan, quirk). Game Informer's retrospective mentions "forgetful wizards" as a signature personality of the original (see 2.4).
- **Barbarians** "equate armor with fear and weakness". Almost always berserk. Strong early; many fans call them broken, enough that a "Barbarian rush" was reportedly banned in multiplayer (fan).
- **Monks.** Cautious, very high willpower, fists only. Fans say their death sound is hard to notice, so they get lost (fan).
- **Healers.** Terrible at fighting, high willpower. Healers follow Warriors around (fan); they turn home after about the third flag when sent far (fan).
- **Gnomes.** Weak, "somewhat pacifistic" builders, with a stench "unmatched in Ardania". When you have Gnomes, new heroes get a slight luck bonus. They breed: one Gnome camp rapidly becomes up to three. If you burn all their homes the Gnomes leave. Fan: they level by building, and "don't evaluate nearby things while building, so they don't flee from phantoms".
- **Dwarves.** Over-engineer: "their basic settlements are tiny fortresses" with automated defences. They rush to help build and repair, and every repair restores a large chunk of hit points.
- **Adepts (Lunord).** Patrol the settlement, avoid heavy armour. Fans: they passively defend remote outposts without flags; fragile at low level; Winged Feet on Tax Collectors "is always fun" and helps them dodge monsters (fan).
- **Rogues.** "Fight with a hand-held crossbow", highest artifice, low willpower. Fans: cheap, flee strong monsters then return to raiding or exploring (fan). Lone Rogues are nearly useless but a squad on forced "defend home" with fire balm is the cheapest strong defence (fan).

## 2.3 The small domestic world

- **Tax Collectors walk the route.** They go from building to building, collect only from those you have left on their route, then return to the Palace or a nearby Guard House. Monsters kill them. The player can set a minimum pickup amount so they ignore small houses (fan: players use 50, 300 or 500).
- **Palace and Guard House henchmen.** The Palace creates Peasants, Tax Collectors and Palace Guards "of their own accord". Guard Houses hire a City Guard who patrols; killed guards are replaced automatically.
- **Peasants** build and repair, run back to the Palace or nearest Guard House for refuge. Fans warn that heroes who try to destroy an unwanted building can kill the Peasants repairing it (fan). Take unwanted buildings off the repair list.
- **Fountains appear on their own** when you have enough Inns, Blacksmiths, Gardens and Marketplaces (the manual: one per five of Fairgrounds, Blacksmiths, Royal Gardens and Marketplaces, though fans say Inns count too). Each adds a Tax Collector. The manual calls them a sign of economic prosperity.
- **Gazebos** appear by themselves when you plant Royal Gardens; free inn for heroes.
- **Caravans** carry Trading Post gold to the nearest Marketplace; the farther they go the more they pay, and the Marketplace level multiplies it. Fan: the first Trading Post yields about 250, the second about 450 (fan). Caravans can be ambushed by Trolls (manual). A fan says enemy kingdoms' caravans can be looted. Players complain of "zero-gold" caravans arriving (fan).
- **Market Day.** An emergency button that dumps gold in the Marketplace at the price of a rest period when it sells and earns nothing.
- **Orphaned heroes.** If their guild or temple is destroyed, they walk to the Palace and leave your kingdom, unless you build a replacement before they arrive, in which case it adopts them (manual).
- **Dead heroes: gravestones.** They stay for a limited time; higher-level heroes keep theirs longer. The dead count toward guild capacity.
- **Rangers can vanish off the map and return.** (manual, see above).
- **A name and a gravestone.** Each hero and monster has a portrait, info page, death scene and gravestone; heroes get randomly drawn names (Wikipedia, Steam). A Steam poster says checking up on a named hero like "Tightpockets, son of Ned" "remains enjoyable" (fan).
- **Sir Argoth of Ravenwood.** A Steam fan recalls this named hero holding the castle gate against a minotaur horde (fan, the hero's name appears in a post; I could not verify it is a stock name).
- **Dying.** Each class says something different with its last breath; a dying Monk's cough; a dragon exploding into lightning is remembered by one fan (fan, plus a search-engine snippet about hero last words).
- **Buildings animate only when occupied.** Fan observation: the world feels alive because buildings are quiet unless someone is inside.
- **Inns** are a safe place to rest far from home and, in the manual, draw heroes who then spend. Fans build "road houses" (guardhouse, inn, trade house) on dangerous frontiers.
- **Fairgrounds tournaments.** You pick melee, archery, magic or combo; heroes whose stat passes a random roll enter. Cute detail: you can close tournaments to force heroes into the field.
- **Library.** Smart heroes visit it to learn spells; Barbarians and Warriors of Discord rarely do. Heroes skip the Library if Inns and Lounges are nearer (fan).
- **Heroes buy potions, rings, enchantments** at the Marketplace, Blacksmith and Wizards Guild; wealthy heroes might not spend. Rogues can poison their weapons.

## 2.4 Voice, tone and dialogue

Voice lines I could confirm only through search-engine summaries or reviewer remarks, so treat as (unsure):
- **Royal Advisor.** Lord Venn (or Ven) Fairweather is the main voice of the game and the character with the most lines; advice is sometimes comically useless. His lines include a "the palace is under siege" cry (fan, blog). The manual's fiction, which you can read, starts with a winded advisor bursting in with news ("Your... Majesty, bad news, come quickly!"), and the manual jokes that the last words of the dying prophet Juleck were to wear a winter coat.
- **Tax Collectors.** A line like "More gold, your Majesty" (fan).
- **Rogues:** "You won't get my gold!" when fighting; "I'm out of here!" when fleeing (wiki summary, unsure).
- **Cultists:** "Wow, pretty star!" on level-up; something about having always wanted an item when finding treasure (wiki summary, unsure).
- **Rangers** on level-up: wild adventure builds character (fan).
- **Monks:** all lines are variants of "ooh" or "ohm" (TV Tropes via summary, unsure).
- Cyberlore's old site offered the voice files as WAVs, including "easter egg" lines, and the manual mentions a Majesty Sound Editor so players could record their own lines and share them.
- Game Informer (about Majesty 2) singled out the narrator and tax collectors as the best voices, and called the rest a step down from the original.

## 2.5 Emergent stories players retell

- The hero who ignores the flag, then suddenly goes.
- The Rogue who walks into the danger area for an explore flag's gold and dies; a second Rogue grabs the item afterwards (fan).
- Three Minotaurs arriving just as you build your first Dwarven Settlement, and the Rangers pulled into the fight by a Ranger Guild you placed next to it (fan).
- A Ranger standing still under attack; Warriors wandering the map at random; Rogues dying far from their guild (Genesis Temple).
- Your Peasants killed by a Rogue trying to demolish the Elven Lounge your Peasants are repairing.
- Heroes heading for the Gambling Hall or Elven Lounge instead of the Troll at the Marketplace.
- A Wizard casting Lightning on Vampires and dying to the reflected bolt from Magic Mirror.
- The Gnome who is never scared of the Black Phantoms because the build routine doesn't check for danger (fan).
- Heroes drawn away by marauding enemy heroes; enemy kingdoms' rogues stealing from you.
- A weak hero beating a Werewolf (fan).
- A Barbarian walking the length of the map following a line of 200-gold flags, dying far from home.
- The Priestess whose skeletons she spawned stand at a distance while she fights, because she moved faster or slower (fan).
- The slow-motion reveal: map dark at start, so unexplored regions feel dangerous (fan blog).
- One writer's summary: ruling Ardania is like being a mother; your subjects drift towards "drinking, gambling and robbing each other's gravestones" (blog paraphrase, the "robbing gravestones" part is that author's joke, not a confirmed mechanic).

## 2.6 What makes it feel alive (synthesis)

Each of these is a small mechanism the sources describe, not a system:
1. **Heroes with a price and a personality.** Same class, different decisions. A hidden loyalty/greed/willpower/intelligence set decides who answers a flag, who steals, who hides in the Inn.
2. **A visible economy chain.** Monsters carry gold, heroes loot it, spend at buildings, return to guild, taxed. Tax Collectors literally walk it and can be killed.
3. **Everyone has a home.** Heroes belong to guilds; destroying the guild makes them wander off to leave the kingdom. Gnomes leave when their homes burn.
4. **Success causes trouble.** Sewers and graveyards are consequences of growth and death.
5. **Decoys.** Guard Houses, Lounges and Gambling Halls absorb monsters; players use them on purpose.
6. **Vice as design.** Elves bring money and decadence together; you choose.
7. **Heroes make you wait.** A flag takes time to be noticed; "it does take a little time between when you place a flag and when heroes begin to take note" (manual).
8. **A monarch's feel.** Players say watching the sprites work without any intervention makes them feel like a king (Steam). Direct control would break immersion (Steam, fan).
9. **A voice.** The Advisor and the heroes' lines give a comedic, warm tone.
10. **Named and buried.** Names, portraits, gravestones, death scenes.

## 2.7 What made Majesty 2 and other clones feel worse

(All fan or review opinion.)
- **Less wanderlust, less personality.** Game Informer: heroes "generally smarter in game terms" but lacking the personality of forgetful wizards and lazy elves. Paradox forum and RPG Codex posters: heroes are "heavily weighted toward staying near their own home" unless chasing a flag; they feel uniform; they "do not express a personality".
- **More micromanagement.** Majesty 2 added defend and fear flags; a blogger likens reward flags to "a less responsive StarCraft"; heroes need more guidance; you build two or three guilds of what used to need one.
- **Hero-spam economy.** Majesty 2 income is spent on new guilds and resurrection; large hero hordes early.
- **Changed character of the world.** One blog: the heroes, Advisor and other good guys look like "selfish (albeit charming) cowards who pretend at heroism", and the Advisor's cynicism about propaganda deflates the heroes.
- **Combat changes.** Accuracy removed so every attack lands; monster hit points inflated (a Majesty 1 boss about 500 HP vs. a Majesty 2 first boss reportedly about 10,000) (fan, one poster).
- **Lost features.** No freestyle or random maps at launch, no co-op; missions repetitive (Wikipedia, IncGamers).
- **Voice acting.** A step down apart from the narrator and tax collectors (Game Informer).
- **The first game's fans' view:** Majesty 1 heroes "rarely need constant incentives or monitoring" and roam freely.

## 2.8 Design hooks for a "minimum bar"

If the designer wants the same spirit, the minimum bar in behaviours (not systems) looks like this:
- Heroes decide by temperament, not by a single "aggro" rule: greed thresholds, willpower, intelligence for libraries, loyalty for theft, fear for fleeing.
- Each hero class has one distinctive habit (Solarii raid lairs and garrison Guard Houses; Rangers wander off the map and come back; Elves play music and gamble; Barbarians berserk; Gnomes ignore danger while building; Priestesses raise skeletons and later take control of undead; Cultists charm animals and turn into them; Rogues steal from everyone including you).
- Monsters have a motive: Trolls want treasure and Marketplaces; Ratmen want tax gold; Rock Golems want buildings; Hell Bears guard dens; Medusae chase heroes; Dragons attack repairers; Vampires reflect spells and kill Wizards.
- Spawners that are tied to the player's own success (sewers, graveyards).
- Bad choices you can make on purpose (gambling hall, Elven bungalow bonus).
- Voice and portrait for every unit; gravestones; a revive puzzle.
